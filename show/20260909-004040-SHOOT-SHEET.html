What is winning right now is a founder on camera, talking to one person, about a problem everybody recognizes, shot well enough that it does not look like an accident. Not a brand video. Not a demo. This sheet turns the research and the six-rule framework into where to stand, what to say in the first three seconds, and eight videos to shoot in order.

The story is the asset. Three 19-year-olds. Two quit their jobs. The third works part-time in a barbershop so the other two can eat while they cold-call barbershops. It is not sustainable unless subscriptions sell, and you should say that on camera, because that sentence is a better hook than anything about software. Every video below is allowed to carry it. The first one is only that.

Hard rule before anything with a phone in frame: Pennsylvania is an all-party consent state and the repo rule is never record a human call. No prospect audio, no prospect name on a screen, ever. Every "call" on camera tomorrow is one of us playing the shop owner (Ant), and the caption says so. The reaction after a real call is fair game. The call itself is not.

What the research says

Pulled from creator-side data and platform write-ups from the last few months. The short version: raw beats polished, the first two seconds decide everything, and the cold-call genre you are already living in is its own viral lane.

  • The decision is made in under two seconds. Instagram measures 3-second hold, and reels that hold above 60% get 5 to 10 times the reach of reels under 40%. The floor for organic reach is a 50% hook rate. Watch time is the top ranking signal, then shares, then saves, then likes.
  • "Yap" is the highest-reach format for personal brands in 2026. Selfie cam, one on-screen hook, subtitles, minimal edit. It works because the feed is flooded with AI and over-produced clips, so rawness reads as real. 30 to 60 seconds is the sweet spot for completion.
  • Young founders are growing on exactly this. Eliza Wu (Corner) went from 10K to 150K to 1M views on a couch with a microphone after studying reels frame by frame. Myles Slayton (Cerca) posts several times a day walking NYC streets and rarely names the app. Lucious McDaniel (BiteSight) hit 18M with his sister saying "my brother built this app." Andreessen's Lester Chen: "the product no longer sells itself."
  • "Cold call with me" is a live TikTok trend. Reps film weekly compilations at a standing desk with a headset. Juliano Massarelli, 18, life insurance, has a 16.3M-view call video. The viewer sees the rep's face, hears both sides, and the pre-call energy and post-rejection reaction are the content. Openers that went viral: "Would it absolutely ruin your day if I told you this was a cold call?" and "Quick question: do you wanna hear what I'm selling, or no?"
  • Series beat one-offs. Storytelling sequences with open loops (build-in-public arcs, "day 1 of building X") and authentic everyday moments are the two formats rated best for founders documenting a new company. "Day 1" and "100 days from scratch" are their own hashtag genres.
  • Camera craft that holds people: eye level, subject off-center looking into the open side of the frame, pulled away from the wall, two cameras on the same side of the room (a medium and a tight) so cuts never feel like a glitch. Cut visuals every 1 to 3 seconds in a yap. Captions matter because a large share of viewers are muted, so the first caption is frequently the real hook.
  • Saves and shares outrank views. Micro-learning and one-idea clips earn saves. Specific and human beats a fake creator persona. Say the search keyword out loud early ("missed calls," "barbershop," "AI receptionist") because reels are now searched.

The six rules, applied to us

Your framework from the 100+ reels that broke a million. The right column is what it means on our set tomorrow. The note that the biggest outliers all had 4, 5 and 6 is the reason topics below are wider than "barbershop software."

1

Visual hook: movement or mid-sentence at frame one, angle change at 0:02–0:03

Ours: every clip starts already talking or already walking. No snpz logo, no "so." B-cam cut lands between second 2 and 3, every single video. If we only have one camera, the second angle is a punch-in in the edit (shoot 4K, crop to 1080).

2

Format: polished yap

Ours: raw stream-of-consciousness delivery, but a real location (a barbershop chair, the desk with the dial screen, the car), a real camera or the good phone, and graphics reinforcing the number being said. Not a scripted read.

3

Text hook is specific, pain- or identity-oriented

Ours: never "how to start a business." Always "the kind of guy who…" or "the call you missed at 2:14 cost you…". Every hook below is written this way.

4

Broad TAM topic

Ours: the video is about money, phones, pride, being 20-something and starting something, getting told no. The barbershop is the setting, not the subject. A barber, a plumber and a college kid should all stay.

5

Never "hi guys." Say "you."

Ours: the first spoken word in every clip is "you," "your," or a question aimed at one person. Line 2 of the sales script already does this: "How many calls do you think you miss a week?" That is a hook, not just a script line.

6

Text hook readable at a glance, never under the UI

Ours: black box, white bold type, upper-middle of frame, on screen from frame one, stays until the angle change. Safe zone diagram in the delivery section.

The room: six camera positions

Will's video from the high front corner, Sept 8. One rectangle, window and door on the far wall, three desks, a whiteboard by the door, a purple LED strip along the ceiling line, dark floor. Everything gets shot here except V0 and V6. Positions are numbered on the map; the setups below say which ones each video uses.

The purple is a brand and a problem. Keep it for position 1 wides and B-roll: nobody else on the feed has this room, and the overhead corner shot with someone looking up is a pattern interrupt on its own. But purple light on a face reads as sick, and a talking head under it will not hold. For every clip at positions 2 and 3, put one white light on the face (a desk lamp bounced off the wall, a ring light, or open the curtain and face the window during the day) and dim the strip to 20 percent or set it warm white. Face lit white, room glowing purple behind. That is the look.

  • Position 1, the corner. Phone taped or clamped high where you shot the video. This is the opener for the "three of us" wide and the day-1 series: someone mid-stride, someone typing, someone looking up at it. Angle change at 0:02 is a cut from here down to position 2. Same lens height every day so the series matches.
  • Position 2, A-cam. Eye level on a tripod near desk 2, shooting toward the far wall. Pull the subject chair into the middle of the room so the window, door and LED line are five feet behind, soft. Sit on the right third of the frame looking left into the space. The purple line across the top of frame is the signature.
  • Position 3, B-cam. Small tripod on the desk 3 edge, 30 degrees right of A, chest-up. Same side of the room as A. Open on this, cut to 2 at 0:02–0:03.
  • Position 4, the calendar. Over the shoulder of whoever sits at desk 3, the booking calendar with its coloured blocks filling half the frame. Blur or use a demo shop's calendar. This is the money B-roll: the thing the receptionist fills.
  • Position 5, the whiteboard. Stand at the door, shoot the board on the right wall for Setup D. Marker already moving at frame one.
  • Position 6, the doorway. Phone at eye level just outside the door looking in. Walk in mid-sentence, straight at the lens, drop into the chair: movement at frame one, then cut to position 2.
  • Housekeeping before the wide: shoes, cans, cooler and the water bottle out of frame. Three people at three desks, the middle of the floor clear. The boom mic on desk 2 records every talking head; run it to whichever camera is closest or record on the PC and sync.

Camera setups: where to shoot from

Four setups cover the nine videos. Set each up once, shoot everything that uses it, then move. That order is the shoot order in the next section. Setup A in this room is positions 2 and 3; Setup B is 2, 3 and 4; Setup D is 5; V0 is at Grady's shop and V6 is outside.

SETUP A

Polished yap, two angles, same side

Where to stand

  • Subject 5 feet or more off the background so it goes soft. Any distance adds separation.
  • A-cam at eye level, 6 to 7 feet back, medium shot (waist up). Subject sits on one third of the frame, empty space on the side they look toward.
  • B-cam 30 degrees off to the same side, tighter (chest up), a touch above eye level. Same side as A so the cut does not flip the room.
  • No wide lens close to the face. Back up and zoom in instead.

The angle-change rule

  • Open on B-cam (tight, already talking). Cut to A-cam at 0:02–0:03. Then alternate every 2 to 4 seconds or on every new idea.
  • One camera only: shoot 4K on A, use a 1.4× punch-in as B in the edit.
  • Hands in frame. Point at the text hook. Lean in on the number.
  • Roll 10 seconds of the subject silent and looking at the lens for a cover shot.

Location options

  • Default: the work room, positions 2 and 3, chair in the middle, purple line behind, white light on the face. For V0 only: the barbershop where Grady works, in his chair, Grady cutting soft-focus behind you. Ask his boss today; if it's a no, V0 is shot in the room from position 1 down to 2 with Grady's clippers on the desk.
SETUP B

The cold-call desk

Where to shoot from

  • Cam 1, face: on the desk at eye level, phone-width away, tight. This is the reaction camera and the one that goes viral. Never turn it off between takes.
  • Cam 2, over the shoulder: behind and above the caller, 45 degrees, showing the back of the phone and the room. Not the screen.
  • Cam 3, top-down (optional): phone propped over the desk looking straight down at the notepad, the tally of calls, the hand hitting dial.
  • Headset or phone to the ear, standing desk if you have one. Movement between calls is part of the genre.

What is on the desk

  • A paper tally: CALLS / OWNERS / NO / CALLBACK. Update it on camera. Numbers are hook material.
  • The dial page on the laptop, screen angled away from every camera. Blur any monitor that makes it into a shot.
  • The script on one card: "How many calls do you think you miss a week?" Visible, handwritten.

The call itself

  • Role-play only. Grady or Aiden plays Ant from the Sunday rehearsal, off-camera, on speaker. Caption: "re-enacted from a real call."
  • Real calls: camera on the face before and after, phone audio not recorded, and the video is the debrief ("he just said…").
SETUP C

Walk and talk

Where to shoot from

  • Phone on a short selfie stick or held at arm's length, lens at eye level or slightly above, subject on the left third, street or shopfronts sliding past on the right.
  • Walk toward the camera (second person walking backward filming) for the B angle. Cut between the two.
  • Golden hour or full daylight. Face the sun, not away from it.

Why it works

  • Movement at frame one for free. Location changes every second. This is the Myles Slayton format and needs zero setup.
  • Best for the "starting a business at 20-something" and "what kind of person" topics, which are the widest ones.
  • Walking past barbershops in town with the door visible is the location pattern interrupt.
SETUP D

Whiteboard / napkin math, top-down

Where to shoot from

  • Phone clamped directly above a table, or over the shoulder at a whiteboard. Hand and marker in frame, face out of frame. Voice over the top.
  • Second angle: A-cam on the face for the punchline only ("that's a man who found another barber").

Use for

  • The money video. Write the math as you say it: missed calls a week, times the value of a client who never comes back, versus the monthly price. The number appearing on paper is the graphic.
  • Anything with a comparison ($79 vs $75.99, 100 callers, 50 cents).

Five a day: the slots

Five posts a day is a production line, not five ideas. Same five slots every day, same positions, same scorecard. A video ships only if it hits at least four of the six rules. Rules 4, 5 and 6 are the cheap ones (topic, "you," readable text) and the ones the outliers all had, so a script that misses one of those gets rewritten, not posted.

Using the references without becoming a quote page

  • One reference per video, one sentence, then what you did with it. "Hormozi's line is volume negates luck. So we're doing 500 calls before we let ourselves judge whether we're good." The second sentence is the video. The first is the hook.
  • Paraphrase and attribute; don't perform the quote. "Goggins' whole thing is…" beats a word-for-word reading you may get wrong. Never cut their clips into yours: the originality score punishes it and it is not your footage.
  • Hormozi = volume, reps, "you're not bad, you're early." Use him for the count, the calls, the not-good-yet honesty.
  • Goggins = doing the thing nobody wants to do, on repeat, until it stops hurting. Use him for the told-no videos, the 40 rejections before lunch, the callus.
  • The Wolf of Wall Street = the phone-room energy, minus the crime. Say the minus out loud every time: "the energy, not the lying." It is a sharper hook and it is your honesty floor on camera. Ant's line ("the guy with an answer for everything is lying") lives here.
  • The leap. "If you're waiting to know what to do, you'll wait forever. You have to be in the room where you find out." That is the wide-topic slot, and the one that gets shared by people who will never buy a receptionist.

On recording calls to people in one-party states: that plan does not make it safe. Pennsylvania's wiretap law applies to the recording itself, and the recording happens here, on your desk, in PA. The usual lawyer advice is that the stricter state wins. Two routes are clean: (1) ask on the call, "Mind if I record this one? We're filming our first week," and a yes is consent in every state and a great four seconds of video; (2) role-play with one of you as the owner, captioned as re-enacted. Grady owns compliance and this is attorney-question territory (Part 5 of the cold-call rules). Until a human rules on it, the repo rule stands: no call audio without a yes on tape.

Spare hooks from the story, for the ones you shoot on the fly

All pain- or identity-shaped, all "you," all wide. Pick one, say it mid-sentence, cut at 0:03.

  • "My cofounder cuts hair part-time so the other two of us can cold-call barbershops. You'd do the same, or you wouldn't."
  • "Your barber misses a call at 2pm. The kid who built the thing that answers it is 19 and hasn't paid himself yet."
  • "It's not sustainable. That's the point. Sustainable is a job."
  • "Nobody picks up at 11pm. Ours does. It's the only employee we can afford."
  • "The type of person who quits at 19 is not the type of person you think."
  • "We have until the savings run out. Here's the number." (Setup D, write it.)

Things to do and say that keep people watching

  • Start mid-thought. Roll the camera, count three in your head, then say the line as if you've been talking already. Cut the first second in the edit.
  • Hold the hook with your hand. Point at the text box as you say it. Eyes go where hands go.
  • One idea per clip. If there's a second idea, it's the next video. Micro-learning earns saves.
  • Say the number, show the number. Every dollar figure or count gets a graphic in the same second it's spoken.
  • Turn on the pivot early. The "but here's the thing" needs to land by 0:10 to 0:12 in a 45-second clip. If hold rate dies in the middle, the pivot was too late.
  • Leave the pause in. One second of silence after the punchline reads as confidence and pulls the eye back to the screen.
  • Answer the objection before they type it. "And yeah, it says it's a demo in its first sentence" kills the "AI is creepy" comment.
  • End on a question or an open loop. "Which one are you." "Same shop, next week." Never "follow for more." Completion and replays are the signal; the loop earns both.
  • Say the search words out loud early. "barbershop," "missed calls," "cold calling." Reels are searched now.
  • Don't know? Say so. The honesty that saved the rehearsal call is the same honesty that earns comments. It also keeps you clear of the no-invented-statistics rule.
  • Reply to every comment for the first hour. The March 2026 two-way signal ranks the next post on how much you answered the last one.

  • 1080 × 1920, 9:16, 30 fps, shoot 4K so a punch-in is a second angle.
  • Text hook in the upper third: black box, white bold sans, three lines max, on from frame one until the first angle change. Never in the top 12% or bottom 27% of the frame, never in the right 16% where the icons sit.
  • Captions word-synced, mid-frame, small. A large share of viewers are muted; the caption is the hook for them.
  • Cuts every 1 to 3 seconds in yap; every beat change at the desk. Both cameras same side of the room.
  • Length 30 to 60 seconds. Under 15 seconds only for the rejection clip. Targets: 50%+ hook rate, 40%+ completion at 30 to 60s.
  • Originality no TikTok watermark on the Instagram upload and no recycled trend audio verbatim; Instagram's originality score cuts reach for both.
  • Blur any screen with a name, number, or shop on it. Fold the list. Blur monitors in the over-the-shoulder.

Tomorrow, before the first take

  • Two phones or a camera plus a phone, both charged, both set to 4K/30. A third phone for top-down at the desk.
  • Lav or shotgun mic. If neither, record voice on a phone in your pocket and sync in the edit. Bad audio kills yap.
  • Tripod or clamp for A-cam, something to prop B-cam at 30 degrees, and a clamp or tape for the corner phone at position 1. Tape marks on the floor for the chair and both tripods so every day matches.
  • One white light for faces (desk lamp bounced off the wall, ring light, or the window by day). LED strip to 20 percent or warm white for every talking head.
  • Grady's boss says yes to 30 minutes in the chair with Grady cutting behind. Ask today. Fallback for V0 is Grady in the frame at the desk, clippers on the table.
  • The paper tally (CALLS / OWNERS / NO / CALLBACK) and the one-line script card, both handwritten, big marker.
  • Whiteboard or a legal pad and a fat marker for V3.
  • Whoever plays Ant has the objections from the rewritten brief in front of them and stays off camera.
  • Confirm with Grady where DNC registration stands so V8 says the true thing about day 1.
